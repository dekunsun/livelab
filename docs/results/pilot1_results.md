## Reading, against the rules fixed before the run

*The tables above are generated and unedited. This section was written after the results were
known, and completed after the owner's checks (below).*

### The picture is used (Pa1, Pa2 met)

With no photograph, both models answered UNKNOWN on **all 100 items**. With the photograph, they
were right on 57 (Opus 5) and 74 (Gemini) of 100. Counting only the items where they committed to
an answer, both are right about three times in four: **57 of 77** and **74 of 99**. What
separates them is abstention. Opus answered UNKNOWN on 23 items with the picture in hand; Gemini
did so on one. Gemini's errors lean one way: it called 18 of 50 abnormal scenes NORMAL.

The C0 result looks like the opposite of this project's first finding, where models almost never
abstained. It is not. There, the missing instrument was stated in the manifest, but the model
had to infer from it that the instruments present could not settle the question. Here the prompt
says outright that the evidence itself is absent. This pilot shows that a stated absence of
evidence is respected. It does not test the inference. *(Corrected 2026-09-21: this first called
phase one's gap "implicit"; the absence was stated there too, and what was left to the model was
the inference.)*

### A description written without the task is worse than the picture (Pa3 not met)

Given a description of the same image, written by a model that was not told what was being
checked, accuracy fell to **50 and 49 of 100**. The fall is almost all on the matched pairs (38%
and 33%, against 57% and 68% with the picture), and it takes one form above all: **scenes
labelled normal were called abnormal**, 15 and 19 times against 11 and 7 with the picture.

I expected the descriptions simply to leave out the thing being checked. Four of Gemini's false
alarms, read in full, do not support that. The descriptions *do* mention the relevant object, and
say something that conflicts with the check. One describes a tube "holding a colourless liquid
roughly two-thirds up" where the check wants an empty tube labelled normal. Another calls a tray
"empty apart from one small teal/green plastic block" where the check wants a mould holding
silicone. Either the describer misread the scene, for instance a reflection read as liquid or a
filled mould read as a block, or the label is lenient. Telling those apart needs the images, and
that is the registered human check still to do.

What this does and does not show:

- It **does** show that, for these models and this task, a generic text description made
  without knowing the check carries less of what the check needs than the image does, when both
  go to the same model.
- It does **not** show that native multimodal beats a specialist vision pipeline. C2 was a
  diagnostic, not a competitive baseline: one describer, not tuned, with no knowledge of the task.

### The step is not ignored (Pa4 met for Opus, not for Gemini)

On the 20 images labelled normal under one step and abnormal under another, Gemini got both
steps right on **14**, and Opus on **10**. Pa4 predicted fewer than 12 for both, on the idea that
a model answering from the picture alone must miss one of each pair. The numbers do not show that
failure. If a model's errors on the two steps were independent, Gemini's 82% accuracy on these
items would give about 67% both-right, and it scored 70%. Opus's 10 is held down by abstentions,
not by wrong answers. Neither model shows a specific blindness to the step.

### The owner's checks, and a second rater

The project owner checked 15 labels and 15 descriptions against their images. They have a
background in materials, have not used these instruments, and judge as a non-expert. The label
check was blind: they saw the step and the check, never the label. GPT-6 Astra, which took no part
in pilot 1, then rated the same 30 blind, after the owner had finished. The owner's records were
committed before Astra ran (`results/review_raw/`, `results/pilot1/second_rater/`).

| Check | Owner | Astra |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Labels: agree / disagree / cannot tell | **14 / 0 / 1** | 9 / 3 / 3 |
| Descriptions with errors that would change a judgement | **8 of 15** | 1 of 15 |
| Descriptions carrying a judgement | 0 | 0 |

**The labels stand.** The registered rule marks a label uncertain only where both raters disagree
with it, and no label met that. The owner disagreed with none.

**The descriptions are where C2 went wrong.** Eight of the fifteen have an error the owner judged
would change the answer, and they cluster on the very details the checks ask about:

- red liquid plainly visible in a tube is described as "no liquid" or "darker below" (images
  1534, 1537, 1555);
- a mould is described as "a cylindrical roller" or "a white cylindrical part" (0625, 0984);
- a bottle with a spout cap is described as uncapped (0719).

This settles the question the first reading left open. C2's false alarms came from the describer
misreading the scene, not from lenient labels. That sharpens Pa3's reading. The loss is not
"text is worse than pictures". It is that **a description written without knowing the check
misses or misreads the details the check depends on**, while the same model, looking at the
picture with the check in hand, did better. This is still not a comparison with a specialist
vision pipeline.

**The second rater errs in the direction of the models under test.** All three of Astra's label
disagreements call an abnormal scene fine. The owner agreed with the label on two of those and
could not tell on the third. On descriptions, Astra called seven of the owner's eight
judgement-changing errors minor or absent. So a model used as the reference would have passed
exactly the failures this pilot is looking for. That is the case, now measured once, for keeping
the reference human here and a model only as a cross-check. Owner and Astra agree on 9 of 15
labels and 8 of 15 description ratings.

Astra's check cost about 30 calls. Its usage is in the records; the spend is small next to the
cap.
